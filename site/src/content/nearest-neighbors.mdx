# Chapter 6 — Ask the neighbours

> *k-nearest neighbours.* Two pastries, two measurements, and a boundary that finally bends.

The wholesaler now drops a mixed crate each morning — croissants and danishes, jumbled — and
Nadia wants them sorted automatically. She has two quick measurements for each: how dark the crust
baked, and how much it weighs. She plots a tray she's already sorted by hand, expecting two tidy
halves.

They're not tidy at all. The two types **interleave** — a light, heavy danish sits right among the
croissants; the clusters curl around one another. It's the swirl she glimpsed back at the proving
bench, and it breaks every tool she owns.

## The wall: the boundary has to bend

Logistic regression and its one-vs-rest cousin can only ever lay down **straight** lines. On
interleaving pastries a straight cut slices through both types at once — there's no angle that
helps. She doesn't need a better line. She needs a boundary that can be *any shape at all*.

## The idea: forget the global rule

So she stops trying to describe the whole tray with one equation. To label a new pastry, she just
finds the handful of already-sorted pastries it most **resembles** — its nearest neighbours — and
goes with the majority. No formula for the boundary; the boundary is simply wherever the examples
say it is. Press play with different crates:

## How it works: lazy learning

The twist is that **k-NN barely trains**. "Training" is one line — just remember the examples:

```ts
train(inputs: Matrix, targets: Matrix) {
    this.inputs = inputs;
    this.targets = targets;
}
```

All the work waits until prediction. For each new pastry, `NearestNeighbors` measures the distance
to every stored example, keeps the closest `k`, and takes their majority vote. Distance is just
Pythagoras, stretched across however many measurements there are:

$$
d^2 = \sum_{j} \big(x_j - x'_j\big)^2
$$

(squared Euclidean by default — and swappable via `setDistanceFunction`).

## The one knob: k

`k` is the only dial, and it trades detail for steadiness:

- **k = 1** — each pastry copies its single closest neighbour. The boundary hugs the data exactly:
  jagged, dotted with little islands, and quick to enshrine one mislabelled outlier.
- **larger k** — more neighbours vote, so the boundary smooths and shrugs off the odd weirdo — at
  the risk of steamrolling a genuinely small pocket.

## Try it yourself

- **Two recipes**, **A ring**, **The swirl** — the very shapes a straight line couldn't touch.
  k-NN follows every curve with no extra machinery, because the boundary *is* the data.
- **Easy day** — when the types do separate cleanly, k-NN handles it as well as any line. It loses
  nothing on the easy cases.
- Drag **k** from 1 upward on **The swirl** and watch the speckled islands melt into smooth arms.

> **Field note — lazy now, pay later.** k-NN does no work up front and *all* of it at prediction
> time: every new pastry is compared against every stored one. Charming for a tray, brutal for a
> catalogue of years. And it trusts raw distance completely — pile on more measurements and
> "nearness" turns to mush, with no way for the model to learn which measurements actually matter.

## What broke

k-NN bends around anything you can plot. But Nadia's next headache isn't on a tray — it's her
**inbox**. Hundreds of messages pile up overnight: real reservations, furious complaints, and
spam dressed up as both. She wants them triaged before she's had coffee.

There's nothing to plot. No crust-darkness, no weight, no distance to take — just **words**. Not
one tool in the Ledger so far can read a sentence. She's going to need a completely different way
to decide.
