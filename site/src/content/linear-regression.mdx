# Chapter 1 — How many croissants?

> *Linear regression.* Nadia's first morning running **The Drifting Leaf**, and the first
> question every café has to answer before sunrise: **how much do we make today?**

It's 5:40am. Tomás, the flour supplier, is already leaning on the counter waiting for the day's
order, and the ovens are still cold. Nadia has to commit to a number of croissants **now** —
before a single customer walks in.

Get it wrong one way and trays of unsold croissants hit the bin at close: money, literally, in
the trash. Get it wrong the other way and she sells out by nine, turning regulars away to
Cogwheel Coffee across the street. The whole café balances on this one guess, made fresh every
single morning.

## The wall: "just bake the average"

Her aunt's method, scrawled inside the till: *bake the average of the last few weeks.* Eighty a
day. Simple, and it feels safe.

It isn't. Eighty is **right on average and wrong every single day.** On a cold, dead Tuesday she
bins thirty. On the first warm Saturday of spring she's empty by 9:15 with a queue out the door.
Averaging throws away the one thing Nadia already suspects matters: *it sells more when it's
warm.*

Press **Train** below, but watch the very first instant — the orange line **starts flat.** That
flat line is a single constant guess for every temperature: it *is* Nadia's "bake the average."
The faint vertical lines are the daily misses one number is stuck with.

A single number can't lean. To do better, Nadia needs something that **bends with the
temperature** — a rule that says *the warmer it is, the more I bake.*

## The idea: draw a line, not a dot

Instead of one height, she sketches a **line** across the scatter of past days — cool mornings on
the left, warm ones on the right, sales climbing up the page. A line in one variable is just:

```
sold = slope · temperature + intercept
```

Two numbers to pin down: the **slope** (how many extra croissants per degree) and the
**intercept** (the baseline on a mild day). Find the best two and she has a smarter answer for
*every* temperature — not one number for all of them.

That's the whole of the library's
[`LinearRegression`](https://github.com/erikgerrits/machine-learning/blob/master/src/lib/machine-learning/supervised/LinearRegression.ts):
a plain matrix product of the (bias-enriched) inputs with the weight vector.

```ts
protected predictFromEnrichedInputs(inputs: Matrix) {
    return Matrix.multiply(inputs, this.getHypothesis());
}
```

(The "bias-enriched" trick: a leading column of `1`s is glued onto the inputs so the intercept
becomes just another weight, folded into the same multiply — see `Regression.enrichedWithBiases`.)

## What makes one line better than another?

Nadia needs a score for *how wrong* a line is. For each past day she takes the **miss** — the gap
between the line and what actually sold — squares it, and averages over all the days. That's the
**mean squared error**:

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \big(\hat{y}_i - y_i\big)^2
$$

The $\sum$ just means *add it up over all $m$ days*. Squaring does two useful things: it makes
every miss count as positive (over or under, a miss is a miss), and it punishes one big blunder
far more than a handful of small ones. That's the number falling in the **Loss** panel — the
squared length of every one of those vertical residual lines, added together.

## How the line finds its place: rolling downhill

Nadia doesn't solve for the perfect line in one shot. She **nudges** it. Picture the MSE as a
valley: every possible (slope, intercept) is a spot on the hillside, and the height is how wrong
that line is. Training starts at the flat line — slope and intercept both zero — and takes small
steps in whichever direction heads *downhill*, again and again, until it rolls to the bottom.

The library's shared `Regression.train` loop does exactly that: measure the error, turn it into a
gradient (the downhill direction), step.

```ts
const errors = predictions.subtract(targets).transpose();
const gradient = errors.multiply(inputs).transpose();
this.hypothesis = Matrix.subtract(this.hypothesis, gradient.multiply(learningRate / exampleCount));
```

The **learning rate** is the size of each step. Too small and the line crawls toward the data;
too large and it overshoots and lurches past the bottom. Drag it up on the **Summer surge**
dataset to feel both failure modes.

## Try it yourself

- Hit **Train** and watch the flat "average" line **tilt** until it follows the trend — that's
  Nadia's eighty-a-day becoming a real forecast.
- Switch to **Chaotic week**: the line still finds the best *average* trend, but the MSE never
  reaches zero — when a street fair drowns out the weather, no line can thread every point.
- Try **Hot cocoa**: the slope goes *negative*. The very same machinery learns "warmer means
  fewer" without ever being told which way to lean.
- Lower the **learning rate** and watch the line approach its final angle in slow motion.

> **Field note — beat the baseline.** Before trusting any model, race it against the dumbest one
> you can write: *predict the average.* That flat line is the bar to clear. A model that can't
> beat it isn't earning its keep — and here, the tilted line clears it comfortably.

## What broke

Linear regression hands Nadia a *number*, and it already beats "eighty a day." But watch the
misses on a busy week: a warm **Saturday** and an equally warm **Tuesday** sell wildly
differently — yet they share the same temperature, so her line gives them the identical forecast
and is wrong on both.

Temperature is only *one* clue. The receipts hold more — the day of the week, the crowd on the
pavement, the festival down the road. Tomorrow she'll teach the line to listen to **all of them
at once.**
