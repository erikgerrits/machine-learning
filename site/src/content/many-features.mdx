# Chapter 2 — More than one clue

> *Multiple linear regression.* Nadia's croissant line works — and then a single warm Saturday
> blows a hole in it.

The temperature line from Chapter 1 is earning its keep. But after a fortnight Nadia notices a
pattern in its *misses*, and it nags at her. Two Saturdays in a row, the same mild 18°, and she
sold out by ten. The Tuesday in between — also 18° — she binned a tray. Her line, seeing the same
temperature, gave all three days the **same** forecast. It was high on the Tuesday and low on the
Saturdays. Wrong, predictably, in a way that has nothing to do with the weather.

## The wall: one clue can't tell days apart

Temperature is *a* signal. It is not the *only* signal. Weekends are busier. A festival on the
high street empties her shelves. None of that reaches a model whose only input is the thermometer
— so it splits the difference and is gently wrong on every day that isn't average.

Below is the fix in motion. Start with only **Temperature** ticked: this is Chapter 1's model,
drawn a new way. Each dot is a day, placed at *(what actually sold, what the model predicted)*. A
perfect model would land every dot on the dashed diagonal — predicted equals actual. With
temperature alone, the dots sit in a loose, fat cloud: the model simply can't explain why two
18° days sold so differently.

## The idea: add up several weighted clues

Instead of one input times one slope, Nadia lets the model weigh *several* inputs at once. Each
feature gets its own **weight** — its own knob saying how much that clue matters — and the
prediction is their sum, plus the baseline:

$$
\hat{y} = b + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_k x_k = b + \sum_{j=1}^{k} w_j x_j
$$

A positive weekend weight nudges Saturday's forecast up; the temperature weight still handles the
weather; foot traffic catches the festival. One day, many clues, one number out.

## How it works: a page of measurements

This is where the library's `Matrix` earns its name. Stack the days into a table — **one row per
day, one column per feature** — and the prediction for *every* day at once is a single matrix
multiply. It's the exact same line of `LinearRegression` from Chapter 1:

```ts
protected predictFromEnrichedInputs(inputs: Matrix) {
    return Matrix.multiply(inputs, this.getHypothesis());
}
```

Nothing changed. In Chapter 1 `inputs` was an `m × 1` column and the hypothesis held two numbers
(bias + slope). Now `inputs` is `m × k` and the hypothesis holds `k + 1` — and the multiply
already does the weighted sum for all of them. Gradient descent trains the extra weights with the
very same loop; there are just more knobs to turn.

The **Recipe** panel shows those knobs as they settle. Let it run on all three features and the
numbers drift toward the hidden truth the data was built from: a baseline around **80**, about
**+22** on weekends, a steady lift per warm degree, and a smaller one for a busy pavement. The
model is reverse-engineering Nadia's café from its receipts.

> **Field note — keep features on the same scale.** Gradient descent takes one step size for
> *all* the weights. If temperature were in tenths and foot traffic in raw counts of thousands,
> the same step would be far too big for one and far too small for the other, and training would
> crawl. The cure is to rescale every feature to a comparable range first — here they already are.

## What broke

Three clues beat one. So Nadia does the obvious thing: if a few features help, surely *more* must
help more. She pulls every column she can find out of the receipts — the date, the moon phase,
the number of pigeons on the awning — and feeds them all in.

On last month's data, the model becomes *uncanny*. Nearly every dot snaps onto the diagonal. She's
thrilled — right up until next week arrives and the forecasts are worse than the plain temperature
line ever was. Somewhere between "more clues" and "too many," her model stopped learning the café
and started **memorising** it.
