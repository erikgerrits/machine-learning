# Chapter 4 — Will it rise?

> *Logistic regression.* Nadia's models predict numbers beautifully. Bea asks her a question that
> has no number in it.

Bea, the baker, jerks a thumb at a tray of dough proving in the cellar. "Worth firing the oven
for, or not?" A good batch baked is a morning's sales. A dud baked wastes flour, gas, and an
hour of oven Nadia doesn't have; a good batch *binned* throws away the same. It's a clean
**yes/no** — and it's exactly the shape of question her regression line can't answer.

She has a hunch the answer is written in two things she can measure the moment the dough goes
down: how **warm** the proving spot is, and how **fresh** the yeast is.

## The wall: a line is the wrong shape for yes/no

Her instinct is to reuse what works. Label every past batch — **1** for rose, **0** for flopped —
and fit a regression line, then call anything above `0.5` a "rise." It falls apart immediately:

- The line doesn't stop at 0 and 1. A gloriously warm, fresh batch scores **1.4** — *one-point-four
  of a rise*, which is meaningless.
- One weird batch far from the rest — a fluke flop on a perfect day — **tilts the entire line** and
  flips calls on batches nowhere near it.
- "0.73" isn't a *probability*; it's just where a straight ramp happened to cross.

She doesn't need a number that drifts off to infinity. She needs a **confidence** — pinned between
0 and 1, near-certain at the extremes, genuinely unsure in the middle.

## The idea: squash the line into a probability

So she takes the same weighted sum as before and bends it through an **S-curve** — the
**sigmoid** — which flattens any input into the range `(0, 1)`:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

Now the output reads as *"probability this batch rises."* To make the call, threshold it at `0.5`.
The dividing line in feature space — where confidence is exactly 50% — is the **decision
boundary**. Press **Train** and watch it settle between the flops and the rises:

## How it works: one extra squash

The remarkable part is how little changes. It's the *same* weighted sum, the *same*
gradient-descent engine from the last three chapters — with one squash bolted onto the end. That's
the entire difference from linear regression in the library's source:

```ts
protected predictFromEnrichedInputs(inputs: Matrix) {
    return Matrix.multiply(inputs, this.getHypothesis()).transform(z => 1 / (1 + Math.exp(-z)));
}
```

The yardstick changes too. Squared error is wrong for probabilities, so logistic regression
minimises **cross-entropy**, which barely shrugs at confident-and-right and screams at
confident-and-wrong:

$$
L = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\,y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\,\Big]
$$

That's the curve in the **Loss** panel. The boundary itself is still a single straight line — its
strength, and soon its limit.

## Try it yourself

- **Tried & true** — flops and rises sit in separate corners. The boundary slides cleanly between
  them: nearly every call correct.
- **Borderline batches** — the two clouds overlap. No straight line can be perfect here, and that's
  the point: near the boundary the model reports something like 55% — an honest *coin-flip*, which
  is far more useful than a smug wrong "yes."
- **Two methods** — two proofing styles curl around each other. The line catches most batches but
  can't bend to follow the curve. Hold that thought; Nadia will need a very different kind of
  boundary later.

> **Field note — 0.5 isn't sacred.** The threshold is a business decision, not a law. If binning a
> good batch barely costs Nadia anything but baking a dud ties up her only oven all morning, she
> should only fire on, say, 80% confidence. Move the threshold to match what a mistake actually
> costs — the model hands you a probability; *you* choose where to cut it.

## What broke

Rise or flop — the yes/no machine handles it. Then Bea, wiping her hands, mentions she doesn't
actually sort batches in two. She sorts them in **three**: under-proofed, just-right, and
over-proofed — and each one heads to a different station.

A single line draws *one* boundary: this side yes, that side no. It has no way to carve a plane
into three. Nadia's going to need more than one line at once.
