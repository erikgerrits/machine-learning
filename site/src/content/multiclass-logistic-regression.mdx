# Chapter 5 — Three stations

> *Multiclass logistic regression.* A batch isn't rise-or-flop. Bea runs three stations, and
> Nadia's one-line model can only ever point two ways.

Bea's bench has three trays going at once. **Under-proofed** dough goes back to prove longer.
**Just-right** heads straight to the oven. **Over-proofed** gets knocked back and repurposed into
focaccia. Three destinations — and Nadia's shiny yes/no classifier from Chapter 4 can sort a batch
into exactly *two*.

## The wall: one line, two sides

A logistic boundary slices the plane into two halves: one side "yes," the other "no." That's all a
single line can ever do. To send batches to three stations she'd need to carve the plane into
**three** regions — and no single straight cut makes three pockets.

## The idea: ask three yes/no questions

She doesn't need a new kind of model. She needs her old one, run **three times**. Train one
detector per station:

- *"Under-proofed, or not?"*
- *"Just-right, or not?"*
- *"Over-proofed, or not?"*

For a new batch, ask all three and hand it to whichever is **most confident**. That's
**one-vs-rest**, and it turns three binary classifiers into a three-way sorter. Press **Train** and
watch the plane carve into stations:

## How it works: one-hot targets and argmax

Each batch's label becomes a row with a single 1 — `[1,0,0]` for under, `[0,1,0]` for just-right,
`[0,0,1]` for over. Training spins up one `LogisticRegression` per column and trains each on its
own slice — that class versus everyone else:

```ts
this.logisticRegressions.forEach((classifier, i) =>
    classifier.train(inputs, targets.getColumn(i)),
);
```

To predict, every classifier reports a probability, the columns are stacked back together, and the
winner is the **argmax** — the station whose classifier is most sure:

```ts
predictions.getMaximumRowIndeces(); // index of the largest value in each row
```

That argmax is exactly what colours each cell of the map. Because every underlying classifier still
draws a *straight* line, the regions meet along straight seams — the plane carved into wedges.

## Try it yourself

- **By the clock** — proving time runs bottom to top, so the three stages stack in horizontal
  bands. Two straight seams split them almost perfectly.
- **Distinct batches** — the outcomes cluster in separate corners; one-vs-rest slots clean wedges
  between them.
- **Swirl** — now the three outcomes spiral around the proving sweet-spot. The straight seams can
  only *roughly* trace the curved borders, and a handful of batches near the centre get misfiled.

> **Field note — one-vs-rest vs. softmax.** This library trains three *independent* yes/no
> classifiers, so their confidences don't add up to 100% — you just take the largest. A true
> *softmax* classifier would couple them into one model whose three probabilities sum to one.
> One-vs-rest is simpler and works well whenever the classes part along roughly straight lines.

## What broke

Stations sorted — as long as the outcomes separate along straight seams. The **Swirl** already
showed the cracks: where classes coil around each other, no number of straight lines can trace the
border.

And Nadia's next sorting job is all curve. She wants to label new pastries by eye from a couple of
measurements, but the types interlock so tightly that every straight seam mangles them. She's going
to have to give up drawing global lines altogether — and instead judge each new pastry by the
company it keeps.
