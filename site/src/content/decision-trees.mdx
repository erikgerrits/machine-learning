# Chapter 8 — A rule you can defend

> *Decision trees.* Every model so far hands Nadia an answer. None of them hand her a *reason*.

The argument is still going at the counter. A regular's latte came out cold after a long wait, and
he wants it comped; the new hire doesn't know the rule and looks to Nadia. Her spam filter could
tell her the email's 72% a complaint — useless here. What she needs is a **policy**: something the
new hire can apply the same way every time, and that she can show the customer without it sounding
made up on the spot.

## The wall: a probability isn't a policy

Logistic regression gives her a boundary equation. Naive Bayes gives her odds. A neural net would
give her a shrug-shaped function. None of them is a *rule a person can follow*. You cannot pin
"0.72" to the staff-room wall, and you cannot read a weight vector aloud to an annoyed customer.

She needs the decision in a shape humans actually use: a short flowchart of yes/no questions.

## The idea: split, then split again

So she builds one from the past cases. Take every order she's ever comped or not, and find the
single question that best **separates** them — say, *"was the wait long?"* Within each branch, ask
the next best question, and the next, until each little group is basically all-comp or all-not.
What you're left with reads straight off as rules. Pick a policy and watch the tree carve the map —
the **rulebook** panel is the actual learned policy in plain words:

## How it works: pick the cleanest cut

"Best question" needs a number. The tree measures a group's messiness with **Gini impurity** —
zero when every case agrees, highest when it's a 50/50 toss-up:

$$
G = 1 - \sum_{c} p_c^{\,2}
$$

```ts
function gini (distribution) {
    return 1 - distribution.reduce((sum, probability) => sum + probability * probability, 0);
}
```

At each node it tries every feature and every threshold, and keeps the cut that drops impurity the
most:

```ts
const gain = parentImpurity - weightedImpurity;
if (gain > 0 && (best === undefined || gain > best.gain)) {
    best = { featureIndex: feature, threshold, gain };
}
```

Then it recurses — stopping when a group is pure, too small, or the depth cap is hit:

```ts
if (depth >= this.maxDepth || indices.length < this.minSamplesSplit || distribution.some(p => p === 1)) {
    return { distribution };
}
```

Because every question is "is this one feature above a cut?", the regions are always
**axis-aligned rectangles** — that's the blocky look in the map, and why a curved border only ever
becomes a staircase of little boxes.

## Try it yourself

- Read the **rulebook** — that *is* the model. No other algorithm in the Ledger lets you do that.
- Switch to **Quadrants** ("comp when exactly one thing went wrong"). A straight line was helpless
  here back in Chapter 4; the tree nails it with two cuts.
- Drag **Max depth**. At depth 1 the policy is too crude (it underfits); crank it to 8 and watch it
  grow a tiny box around every single noisy case — the leaf count balloons and it's **overfitting**
  again, exactly like Chapter 3.

> **Field note — depth is the regularizer.** A tree with no depth limit will memorise its training
> set perfectly, noise and all. Capping the depth (or demanding a minimum number of cases per split)
> is how you keep the policy simple enough to generalise — the same bias-for-simplicity that
> regularization bought the regression models, in a different costume.

## What broke

The rulebook is a triumph of clarity. It's also alarmingly **jittery**. Reseed the data — same
policy, a fresh sample of cases — and watch the whole tree rearrange: a different first question, a
different shape. One mislabelled case near a boundary can flip an entire branch.

A policy that reshuffles itself every time you glance at fresh data is hard to trust. One tree is a
single, opinionated, slightly nervous judge. Nadia's next idea is the obvious one: don't ask a
single nervous judge — ask a whole *crowd* of them, each shown a slightly different slice of the
evidence, and let them vote.
